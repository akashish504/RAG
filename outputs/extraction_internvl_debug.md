## Slide 5: (untitled)

- WHY IS GENDER IMPORTANT?
- THE CHALLENGE
- THE OPPORTUNITY
- Women comprise, on average, 43% of the agricultural labor force in developing countries, ranging from 20% in Latin America to more than 60 % in Sub-Saharan Africa1.
- Women and girls face limited access to key enablers that ensure an inclusive agriculture transformation
- Key enablers
- Skills: Literacy rates for rural women can be as low as 30% in some African countries5 which can hinder their use of more complex digital platforms
- Time: Globally, women spend an average of 3X on unpaid care work and domestic work than men, reducing the time they have available to do other activities, like attend a training6
- Mobility: In many countries, women cannot make decisions about where to go, limiting their access to markets and services delivered outside of the home (e.g., in Nigeria, only 8% of married women can make personal decisions about visits to family)7
- Closing gender gaps in agriculture could generate significant gains for the agriculture sector, as well as broader socio-economic outcomes for households:
- Productivity drivers
  - Inputs
  - Assets
  - Skills
  - Financial services
  - Time
- Market linkages
  - Market intelligence
  - Mobility
  - Buyer linkages
- Enabling environment
  - Business, policy and legal environment
  - Infrastructure
- Yields: A study of maize farmers in Kenya found that an increase in women’s empowerment by 1% led to a 6.4% increase in maize yield due to improved uptake of farming technologies and services.2
- Food security: Closing yield gaps between men and women could raise total agricultural output in developing countries by 2.5- 4% to feed an additional 100-150 million people3
- Broader socio-economic benefits: Increasing women’s share of household income through supporting their agricultural livelihoods can help drive greater household-level businesses compared to an equivalent increase for men. Increasing women’s income by $10 achieves the same improvements in children’s nutrition and health as an increase to a man’s income of $1104
- 1) CGIAR, “Empowering Africa’s women farmers,” 2019; 2) Gracious M. Diiro, et al, “Women’s empowerment in agriculture and agricultural productivity: Evidence from rural maize farmer households in western Kenya,” 2018; 3) FAO, “The State of Food and Agriculture,” 2011; 4) FAO, “FAO Committee on Food Security”, 2011. 5) Prepared by the SOFA Team and Cheryl Doss, “ The Role of Women in Agriculture,”  2011. 6) IFC, “Investing in Women Along Agribusiness Value Chain,” 2016. 6) Dalberg, “It’s about time: The case for valuing women and girls' unpaid care work,” 2019. 7) The DHS Program, “Nigeria DHS,” 2018.

## Slide 7: (untitled)

- WHAT GUIDELINES CAN YOU FOLLOW WHEN FACILITATING GENDER CONVERSATIONS?
  - Prepare a thoughtful business case and answers to difficult questions. Invest time identifying the business case for a potential partner in advance and practice responses to difficult questions.
  - Celebrate successes while keeping in mind improvement areas. Reinforce what partners are doing well and build connections between strengths and weaknesses to encourage momentum.
  - “Move at the speed of trust”. Introduce sensitive topics (e.g., gender-based violence, salary levels) slowly, taking stock of the relationship to determine whether the partner feels secure enough to engage productively in dialogue.
  - Build on common ground. Frame objectives, questions, and suggestions using familiar examples (e.g., local-level) to establish linkages between abstract concepts and partners’ lived experiences.
  - Suspend judgement. Instead of passing judgement, communicate with respect and open-mindedness, using negative responses as an opportunity for dialogue.
- Sources: WHO, “Gender mainstreaming for health managers,” 2011. Health Policy Project, “A Facilitator’s Guide for Public Health and HIV Programs: Gender & Sexual Diversity Training,” 2015. Quartz, “Gloria Steinem says these are the best guidelines for difficult conversations,” 2018. GuideStar, “Navigating Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Conversations,” 2018. Next Pivot Point, “Gender Equality is a Candid Conversation: 3 Key Attributes to Facilitate THE Conversation,” 2020. LeanIn, “Discussion Guide: Difficult Conversations,” 2013. LivingRoom Conversations, “Conversation Guide: Women, Leadership, And Power,” 2020.

## Slide 8: (untitled)

- WHAT THE GENDER TOOL WILL AND WILL NOT DO?
- The gender tool will:
- Provide prompts to facilitate a conversation with IDH partner(s); the conversation can be structured however makes the most sense (e.g., open-ended questions vs. asking questions verbatim), as long as the relevant information is collected
- Generate a long-list of ideas of potential actions an IDH partner(s) can take
- Collect data on key gender indicators to establish a starting point from which a partner can measure progress from
- The gender tool will not:
- Provide a script that will tell you verbatim what to say to the IDH partner
- Generate an exhaustive list of recommendations that is applicable to every project context or variation or that will ‘solve’ all gender challenges
- Cover all the support or information that IDH provides on gender (e.g., IDH Gender Toolkit, Farmfit Gender Expertise)
